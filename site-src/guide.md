# Guide

This page is the roadmap. It takes you from no Italian at all to reading and watching things made for Italians. Each section is one stage, and each stage builds on the one before it:

1. Learn how Italian spelling maps to sound.
2. Install Anki and Yomitan.
3. Learn a starter vocabulary and the grammar you need to parse simple sentences.
4. Spend most of your time with native Italian, and turn unknown words into cards.
5. Start talking and writing once you understand most of what you hear.
6. Keep going, and raise the difficulty as you improve.

The first three stages take a few weeks. The fourth takes a year or more, and it's where you learn most of your Italian. Tick off each stage in the checklist at the bottom.

## Learn pronunciation and spelling

Italian spelling is regular. Once you know about a dozen rules, you can read almost any word aloud, even one you've never seen. Spend your first few days on these rules and say every example out loud.

### Vowels and single letters

Italian has five written vowels, a, e, i, o and u. Say each one short and clean, without the glide English adds to "no" or "day". Don't reduce an unstressed vowel to the "uh" sound English uses, so the final o in *pronto* stays an o. The letter h is always silent.

### C and g before e and i

C and g each have two sounds, and the next letter decides which one you hear.

- Before e or i, c sounds like the "ch" in "church" and g sounds like the "j" in "jam". Examples are *cena*, *cinema*, *gelato* and *giro*.
- Before a, o, u or a consonant, c sounds like "k" and g sounds like the "g" in "go". Examples are *casa*, *cuore*, *gatto* and *grande*.
- To keep the hard sound before e or i, Italian adds an h. *Chi* sounds like "key", and *ghiaccio* starts with a hard g.
- To keep the soft sound before a, o or u, Italian adds an i that you don't pronounce as its own vowel. *Ciao* starts with the "ch" of "church", and *giallo* starts like "jah".

### Gl, gn and sc

- Gli sounds close to the "lli" in "million", said as one sound. You hear it in *figlio*, *famiglia* and the article *gli*. A few words of Greek origin keep a plain g plus l, such as *glicine*, "wisteria".
- Gn sounds like the "ny" in "canyon". Examples are *gnocchi*, *bagno* and *ogni*.
- Sc before e or i sounds like the "sh" in "shoe", as in *scena* and *uscita*. Before a, o or u, sci gives the same "sh" sound, and the i is silent in words such as *lasciare*. Sch before e or i keeps a hard "sk", as in *schiena* and *scherzo*. Before a, o or u, sc is "sk", as in *scuola*.

### Double consonants

A double consonant is longer than a single one. Hold it for a moment before you release it. This changes meaning. *Nono* is "ninth" and *nonno* is "grandfather". *Caro* is "dear" and *carro* is "cart". *Pala* is "shovel" and *palla* is "ball".

### Written stress

Italian writes an accent only on a stressed final vowel, as in *città*, *più*, *così*, *però* and *caffè*. Most of these are grave accents. A final e with a closed sound takes an acute accent instead, as in *perché* and *poiché*. A few one-syllable words carry an accent to tell them apart from others spelled the same way. *È* means "is" and *e* means "and". *Sì* means "yes" and *si* is a pronoun.

Italian doesn't mark stress anywhere else. Most words stress the second-to-last syllable, as in *parlare*, *amico* and *finestra*. Many don't. *Tavolo*, *telefono* and *macchina* stress the third-to-last syllable, and so does the verb form *parlano*, "they speak". A few pairs differ only by stress. *Àncora* is "anchor" and *ancora* with stress on the o is "still" or "again". Dictionaries print these accents to show you, but ordinary Italian text leaves them out.

### When to check a dictionary

Check the pronunciation of a new word when it has three or more syllables and no written accent, because you can't work out the stress from the spelling. Once you finish the Setup page, Yomitan gives you the IPA transcription and an audio recording for each word you look up. The [Resources](resources.md#pronunciation) page lists Forvo, DiPI Online and other pronunciation tools for words Yomitan misses.

## Set up Anki and Yomitan

Install the two tools the rest of this guide relies on. Anki schedules your flashcard reviews. Yomitan shows a dictionary entry when you hover over a word and saves it as an Anki card in one click.

The [Setup](setup.md) page walks you through every step. By the end of it you will have:

- Anki installed with FSRS turned on and a limit of 10 new cards a day.
- Yomitan installed with the language set to Italian and the `wty-it-en` dictionaries.
- AnkiConnect linking the two programs.
- An Italian note type built from Anki's Basic, with fields for the word, the sentence, the definition, audio and a picture.

Do it now, because the next stage depends on it.

## Learn basic grammar and vocabulary

This stage gives you the common words and enough grammar to see how a sentence fits together. It takes a few weeks. Do both halves at the same time.

### Start a vocabulary deck

The first few hundred words of Italian make up a large share of every sentence you'll read. Learn them from a ready-made deck so that your first episodes and articles aren't all new words.

Use [15000 Italian sentences sorted from easy to hard](https://ankiweb.net/shared/info/1713927804) on AnkiWeb. Its author ranked each sentence by how common its words are and sorted the deck from easiest to hardest, so new words come in slowly as you go. About 10,000 of its 18,900 notes have audio. Download the file, double-click it to import it, and set the deck to 10 new cards a day to match the limit on the Setup page.

The deck has faults. Reviewers on its page report wrong English translations and some bad Italian sentences, and its subdecks are confusingly named. Treat the English as a rough hint. If a sentence looks wrong, check it in [WordReference](https://www.wordreference.com/iten/) and delete the card if it is. Start with the part 1 subdeck, which has audio, and work through it in order.

You don't need to finish it. Once you mine enough cards from your own immersion to fill your daily new-card limit, suspend the rest of the starter deck and give the whole limit to mined cards. The [Immersion](immersion.md#is-mining-words-from-a-dictionary-worth-it) page explains why cards from real content stick better.

If you'd rather not use a shared deck, build a starter set yourself. Pick the easiest content you can find, such as a learner podcast with transcripts or an Italian-dubbed episode of a show you know in English. Mine every unknown word that appears more than once, 10 a day. This is slower for the first two weeks and gives you cards that already mean something to you.

### Learn the core grammar

Before your first native content, learn these topics well enough to recognise them in a sentence:

- **Gender and number.** Nouns are masculine or feminine, and adjectives change to match them. Most masculine nouns end in -o in the singular and -i in the plural. Most feminine nouns end in -a in the singular and -e in the plural.
- **Articles.** The definite articles are *il*, *lo*, *l'*, *la*, *i*, *gli* and *le*, and the indefinite ones are *un*, *uno*, *una* and *un'*. The choice depends on gender, number and the first sound of the next word. That's why you say *il libro* but *lo studente* and *l'amico*.
- **Articulated prepositions.** The prepositions *di*, *a*, *da*, *in* and *su* merge with a following definite article. *Di* plus *il* becomes *del*, *a* plus *la* becomes *alla*, and *in* plus *gli* becomes *negli*. Learn the full table.
- **Present tense.** Learn the regular endings for verbs in -are, -ere and -ire, including -ire verbs such as *finire* that add -isc-, as in *finisco*. Then learn the common irregular verbs *essere*, *avere*, *andare*, *fare*, *stare*, *dire*, *venire*, *potere*, *volere* and *dovere*.
- **Passato prossimo.** This is the everyday past tense. It pairs a present form of *avere* or *essere* with a past participle, as in *ho mangiato* and *sono andato*. With *essere*, the participle agrees with the subject, so a woman says *sono andata*.
- **Pronouns and negation.** Italian often drops subject pronouns, because the verb ending shows who is acting. *Parlo* already means "I speak". Put *non* before the verb to negate it.

Read an overview of each topic once and move on. The [Resources](resources.md#grammar) page lists free grammar references in English and Italian. Wikibooks Italian and Language Transfer both suit a first pass.

### How much grammar is enough

Spend one or two weeks on it, at about an hour a day. Read the explanations, look at the examples and skip the exercises. You're done when you can look at a short sentence and pick out the verb, its tense and what each article and preposition is doing, even if you don't know every word.

You won't remember all of it. Immersion shows you each pattern thousands of times, and that is how it sticks. Later topics such as the *congiuntivo*, the future tense and combined pronouns like *gliel'ho* can wait until you meet them in content. The [Immersion](immersion.md#are-textbooks-bad) page has more on where textbooks help and where they slow you down.

## Consume native content

This is the main stage, and it lasts as long as you learn Italian. Start it as soon as you have a few hundred cards and the core grammar behind you.

Pick things you'd enjoy in English. For the first few hundred hours, the easiest input is a show you've already seen, dubbed into Italian, with Italian subtitles. The [Recommendations](recommendations.md#start-here) page gives three places to start and a longer list sorted by medium and difficulty.

Each day, do your Anki reviews first. Then give at least an hour to content with your full attention, and add listening in the background whenever you can. The [Immersion](immersion.md) page answers the questions that come up in your first months.

When you meet a word you don't know, look it up with Yomitan and add it as a card if it seems worth learning. A word you've seen a few times, or one that blocks the meaning of a sentence, is worth a card. A rare word you'll probably never see again isn't. The [Mining](mining.md) page shows how to do this for web pages, video with subtitles, ebooks and comics.

## Talk and write to Italian speakers

Wait until you understand most of what you hear in ordinary conversation before you practise speaking. As a rough estimate, that takes 1,000 to 1,500 hours of input. The [Immersion](immersion.md#when-should-i-start-speaking-and-writing) page explains why. Two exceptions apply. Read aloud from day one to practise pronunciation, and if you live in Italy or have Italian family, talk to them now.

When you reach that point, start with writing, because it gives you time to find each word. Keep a short diary in Italian. Comment on Italian forums, subreddits and YouTube videos about your hobbies, where people reply because they share your interest and not because you're learning.

Then move to speaking. A conversation tutor is the most direct route. Choose one who lets you talk about things you care about and who corrects the mistakes you repeat, not one who follows a syllabus. The [Immersion](immersion.md#should-i-get-a-tutor-or-take-classes) page covers what to look for.

## What's next

After a year or so of steady immersion, you'll follow most everyday Italian. From there, the work is to widen what you understand.

- **Switch to Italian definitions.** Install `wty-it-it` in Yomitan, as the [Setup](setup.md#install-italian-dictionaries) page describes, and make it your first dictionary. Use [Treccani](https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/) and the other monolingual dictionaries on the [Resources](resources.md#monolingual-dictionaries) page for harder words.
- **Drop subtitles.** Turn them off for easy shows first, then for everything except heavy dialect.
- **Explore regional speech.** Watch shows set in Naples, Sicily or Rome and get used to their accents.
- **Take an exam if you need one.** The [Miscellaneous](miscellaneous.md#certifications) page compares the Italian certifications for study, work and citizenship.

Keep mining and keep your reviews daily.
