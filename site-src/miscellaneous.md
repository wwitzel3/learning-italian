# Miscellaneous

## Why input works

This site asks you to spend most of your time reading and listening to Italian you can mostly follow. This section explains why, which parts of that advice rest on solid research, and which parts researchers still argue about.

### Comprehensible input

The idea comes mostly from the linguist Stephen Krashen. In *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*, published in 1982, and in *The Input Hypothesis* in 1985, he argued that people acquire a language in one way. They understand messages in it. He called those messages "comprehensible input." The best input sits a little above your current level. You know enough of the words and grammar to follow the meaning, and the context fills in the rest. Krashen wrote this as "i+1," where "i" is what you already know. Krashen hosts a free copy of [*Principles and Practice*](https://sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf) on his website.

Krashen also split "acquisition" from "learning." Acquisition is the unconscious process that lets you understand and speak without thinking about rules. Learning is conscious knowledge about the language, such as a conjugation table you memorised. In his model, learned rules act only as an editor that checks your output when you have time to think. They don't turn into fluent speech on their own.

In practice this gives you a simple rule. Spend most of your hours understanding Italian, and treat grammar study and flashcards as tools that make more Italian understandable. That is the plan the [immersion page](immersion.md) follows.

### Learning words from reading

Native speakers know tens of thousands of words, and nobody taught them most of those words one by one. Reading researchers have studied where the words come from. William Nagy, Patricia Herman and Richard Anderson tested 352 school pupils in 1985 on hard words from passages they had read a few days earlier. In ["Learning Words from Context"](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ311465), they reported small but reliable gains at every grade and ability level. The gain from each encounter is tiny. Over millions of words of reading, it adds up.

Two findings from second-language research make this more concrete, and they also show its limits.

- **You need to know nearly every word on the page.** Marcella Hu and Paul Nation found in 2000 that readers of English as a second language needed to know about 98% of the running words in a fiction text to understand it well enough without help. At 98%, one word in fifty is new. Their paper, ["Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension"](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/43), is why this guide front-loads a starter vocabulary deck and recommends easy material first.
- **One book teaches you very few words.** Rob Waring and Misako Takaki gave learners a graded reader and tested them on 25 target words. After three months, the average learner remembered the meaning of about one of them. Words met fewer than eight times were gone. Their study, ["At what rate do learners learn and retain new vocabulary from reading a graded reader?"](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/78), supports reading a lot rather than reading a little.

Put together, these results say that reading does teach vocabulary, but slowly, and mostly for words you meet many times. That is the case for pairing large amounts of input with a flashcard deck for the words you want to keep.

### Spaced repetition

Flashcards work better when you space the reviews out than when you cram them into one sitting. This effect is one of the oldest findings in memory research, going back to Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s. Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues combined 317 experiments in a 2006 review, ["Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks"](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html), published in *Psychological Bulletin*. They found that spacing helps. They also found that the best gap between reviews grows the longer you want to remember something.

Anki schedules each card this way. It shows you a card just before you are likely to forget it, and it stretches the gap each time you remember. The [setup page](setup.md) covers how to configure it.

### What's debated

Researchers on both sides agree that you need a lot of input to learn a language. The argument is over whether input is enough and whether conscious study helps.

- **Output.** Merrill Swain studied Canadian French immersion students who had years of input. They understood French very well but still made many grammar errors when they spoke and wrote. In 1985 she proposed the output hypothesis. It says that speaking and writing push you to notice gaps in what you know. This guide agrees that output matters. It only suggests that you start once you have a base of input to draw on.
- **Noticing.** Richard Schmidt argued in a 1990 paper in *Applied Linguistics*, ["The role of consciousness in second language learning"](https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/11/2/129/163482), that you have to consciously notice a feature of the input before you can acquire it. If he is right, some attention to form helps, and so does looking up a grammar point when a sentence confuses you.
- **Testability.** Kevin Gregg in 1984 and Barry McLaughlin in 1987 argued that "i+1" is hard to measure. Nobody can say exactly what a given learner's "i" is. That makes the strong version of Krashen's theory hard to prove or disprove.

This guide takes a middle position. Input does most of the work. A little grammar study, flashcards and later output speed it up. You don't need to settle the academic debate to follow it. You need an hour or more of Italian you mostly understand, every day, for a year or longer.

## Certifications

You don't need a certificate to learn Italian. You may need one to study at an Italian university, to apply for Italian citizenship, or to prove your level to an employer. Four bodies issue the main exams, and the Italian state recognises all four. In 2013 they formed an association called [CLIQ](https://www.associazionecliq.it/), short for Certificazione Lingua Italiana di Qualità.

All four exams use the CEFR, the Council of Europe's six-level scale from A1 for beginners to C2 for near-native mastery.

| Exam | Issuer | Levels | Notes |
|------|--------|--------|-------|
| [CILS](https://cils.unistrasi.it/) | Università per Stranieri di Siena | A1, A2, UNO-B1, DUE-B2, TRE-C1, QUATTRO-C2 | Also runs a separate B1 Cittadinanza exam aimed at adults applying for citizenship. |
| [CELI](https://cvcl.unistrapg.it/) | Università per Stranieri di Perugia, through its CVCL centre | CELI Impatto A1, CELI 1 A2, CELI 2 B1, CELI 3 B2, CELI 4 C1, CELI 5 C2 | Also has CELI Adolescenti for ages 12 to 18 at A2 to B2, and CELI Immigrati for adult immigrants at A1 to B1. |
| [PLIDA](https://plida.dante.global/it/esami-plida/) | Società Dante Alighieri | A2, B1, B2, C1, C2 for adults | PLIDA Juniores covers A1 to B2 for ages 13 to 18. The adult exam has no A1. |
| [CERT.IT](https://certificazioneitaliano.uniroma3.it/en/about-us/) | Università degli Studi Roma Tre | A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2 | One exam per CEFR level. |

### University admission

Non-EU students applying from abroad normally have to pass an Italian language test at their university. The Ministry of University and Research exempts students who hold a certificate at B2 or above from any of the four CLIQ bodies. A certificate at C1 or C2 also exempts students from the limit on places reserved for them. These rules appear in the ministry's yearly circular on [Studiare in Italia](https://www.studiare-in-italia.it/studentistranieri/). The Perugia centre states on its [CELI page](https://cvcl.unistrapg.it/pagine/esami-celi-generici) that the ministry accepts CELI 3 for university enrolment.

Check the admission page of the university you are applying to as well, since each one publishes its own rules.

### Citizenship

Since a 2018 decree, converted into Law 132 of 1 December 2018, people applying for Italian citizenship by marriage or by residence must prove Italian at B1 or above. The [Italian consulate in Lugano](https://conslugano.esteri.it/news/dal_consolato/2019/01/cittadinanza-italiana-novita-d/) summarises the change. A notice from the [Ministry of the Interior's prefecture offices](https://prefettura.interno.gov.it/sites/default/files/101/2023-11/conseguimento_della_certificazione_di_conoscenza_della_lingua_italiana.pdf) names the four issuers above as bodies whose certificates count. It also accepts certificates from the state's adult education centres, called CPIA, and lists some exemptions.

Any B1 or higher certificate from the four issuers meets the requirement. The CILS B1 Cittadinanza exam targets this use. Its content covers everyday life in Italy, public services and work.

### Work

No single rule covers jobs. Employers and professional bodies set their own requirements. A certificate gives you an official level to put on a CV, and the Perugia centre describes its CELI certificates as usable for work and study. Ask the employer what they accept before you book an exam.

### Choosing one

For citizenship and university admission, all four certificates count equally, so pick based on practical details:

- which exam centres are near you,
- which dates fit your deadline,
- which exam format you prefer after trying the sample papers each issuer publishes.

Each issuer's site lists its exam centres, in Italy and abroad. [Resources](resources.md) lists grammar references that help with exam preparation.

Exam names, levels, fees, dates and legal requirements change. Check the issuer's official page, and the rules of the university or office you are applying to, before you register. The facts above were checked against those pages in September 2026.
